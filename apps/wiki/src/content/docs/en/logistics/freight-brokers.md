A **freight broker** is an intermediary that connects **shippers** (companies with goods to ship) with **carriers** (trucking companies) to facilitate the movement of cargo. The broker does not own transportation assets — it coordinates logistics.

## Role of a Freight Broker

Freight brokers perform three core functions:

1. **Matching**: finding the right available carrier for each shipment.
2. **Negotiation**: securing competitive rates through their network of carriers.
3. **Management**: tracking shipments, handling paperwork, and resolving issues in transit.

## How It Works

1. The shipper contacts the broker with a shipping need (destination, weight, cargo type).
2. The broker finds a carrier with the right capacity and route.
3. A **rate** is negotiated between the broker and the carrier.
4. The carrier delivers the cargo and gets paid by the broker.
5. The broker invoices the shipper at a **marked-up price** — the difference is the broker's margin.

## Benefits

- **For shippers**: access to a vast carrier network without having to find them individually.
- **For carriers**: fill empty trucks, stabilize revenue.
- **For the broker**: commission-based income without investing in a fleet.

## Regulation (United States)

In the U.S., freight brokers must be **licensed** by the **FMCSA** (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration). They must also obtain a **$75,000 surety bond** or equivalent.

## Key Takeaways

- A freight broker is an **intermediary**, not a carrier.
- They own no trucks or ships.
- Their value lies in their **network** and **negotiation skills**.
- They handle documentation and shipment tracking.
- The margin is the difference between the shipper's price and the carrier's payment.
